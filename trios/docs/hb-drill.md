# Heartbeat Drill

1. When a worker is given a task and begins streaming its response, the outside world has exactly one channel of evidence for progress: the bytes arriving on that stream. Between the first token and the last, every second that passes without a new byte is a second the observer cannot account for. The worker might be thinking, fetching, computing, sleeping, dead, or network-partitioned — and from the outside these states are, on their own, completely identical. A silence of ten seconds could be a model warming up its context window; a silence of ten minutes could be a hung socket holding the connection open. There is no field in the silence itself that says which.

2. The instinctive objection is that the connection layer should resolve the ambiguity: if the TCP socket is still open, the worker must still be running. But an open socket proves only that no one has closed it — not that anything on the far end is still alive. A process can freeze, deadlock, or enter an infinite loop without ever sending a TCP RST. The kernel keeps the file descriptor valid; the recv buffer stays empty; the connection reads as healthy from the operating system's perspective. The gap between "the socket is open" and "the worker is making progress" is precisely the gap that needs to be closed, and the socket cannot close it on its own.

3. The mechanism that closes the gap is a periodic progress mark emitted by the worker into its own stream. Every thirty seconds, while the stream is active, the worker writes a short structured note: which task is running, how much wall-clock time has elapsed since the stream started, and how many characters of output have been produced so far. This mark is not a log line in a separate channel — it is placed directly in the stream that the observer is already reading, so that it arrives on the same socket, through the same buffer, with the same delivery guarantees as the work product itself.

4. The choice of thirty seconds is a deliberate trade-off between noise and responsiveness. A shorter interval — say, five seconds — would flood the stream with marks that crowd out the actual content and add parsing overhead on the receiving end. A longer interval — say, five minutes — would leave the observer blind for minutes at a time, defeating the purpose. Thirty seconds is long enough that a worker producing real output will often emit several chunks of content between marks, and short enough that a genuine stall becomes visible within a single interval rather than after a long stretch of anxious waiting.

5. The three fields carried by each mark — task identifier, elapsed time, and character count — are chosen so that a single mark, read in isolation, tells the observer something useful. The task identifier confirms that the worker is still on the assignment it was given, not silently idling or working on something stale. The elapsed time provides a monotonic clock that the observer can compare against the mark's arrival time to detect clock skew or delayed delivery. The character count gives a cumulative measure of productivity: if two consecutive marks report the same character count, the worker has produced nothing between them, even though the stream is notionally still open.

6. Marks are emitted only while the stream is actively flowing. The moment the worker finishes its task — whether by completing the work, encountering an error, or being interrupted — the stream closes and the marks stop. This is not a special case that needs to be coded separately; it falls out of the design naturally. The mark-writing loop is part of the streaming loop, and when the streaming loop exits, the mark-writing loop exits with it. There is no background timer that keeps ticking after the stream has ended, no orphaned heartbeat that misleads the observer into thinking the worker is still alive after it has already stopped.

7. With the marks in place, the interpretation of silence becomes unambiguous. If more than a minute passes with no new content and no new mark on the stream, the observer can conclude that the stream itself has gone quiet — not that logging is disabled, not that the marks are being filtered by some intermediary, not that the worker is working hard but just hasn't written anything yet. The absence of a mark that was due thirty seconds ago is itself a signal, as strong and as legible as any positive byte on the wire. One missed mark is a warning; two missed marks — pushing past the one-minute threshold — is a diagnosis.

8. Remove the marks and the entire edifice collapses. Without them, the observer is back to staring at an empty recv buffer with no way to distinguish a hard-working model that is composing a long internal chain of thought from a process that crashed five seconds after the last byte. Both produce the same observable: silence on the stream. The observer's only recourse is to set an external timeout — say, kill the connection after five minutes of inactivity — and that timeout is a blunt instrument that will kill slow-but-healthy workers alongside genuinely hung ones, with no way to tell the difference in advance. The marks exist precisely so that the observer never has to make this guess.

9. A subtlety worth stating explicitly is that the marks are not heartbeat packets in the networking sense. They do not travel a separate keepalive channel; they are not implemented as WebSocket pings or TCP keepalives. They are ordinary content written into the ordinary stream, visible to the same parser that processes the worker's output. This means they consume bandwidth and parsing attention proportional to their frequency, and it means that any component that filters or transforms the stream must be aware of them and must not strip them out silently. A proxy that drops the marks to "clean up" the output would reintroduce the very ambiguity the marks were designed to eliminate, and would do so in a way that is extraordinarily difficult to debug, because the output would look clean and correct while silently losing its liveness signal.

10. The principle, reduced to its essence, is this: liveness is not a property of a connection; it is a property of evidence. A connection can be open and dead. A process can be running and frozen. The only proof that work is happening is work arriving — bytes, tokens, marks, anything that could not exist unless something on the far end was actively producing it. The heartbeat drill makes that proof continuous and explicit: every thirty seconds, the worker offers a small piece of evidence that it is alive and making progress, and the absence of that evidence is, by design, indistinguishable from death. This is not pessimism; it is engineering. In a distributed system, silence is not absence — it is a signal, and the heartbeat drill ensures that signal is read correctly.
