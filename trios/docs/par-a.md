# Parallel Work

1. Parallel work is the practice of splitting a large task into smaller, independent pieces that can be executed concurrently by multiple workers. Each worker operates on its own portion of the problem without needing to coordinate with others on every step. This approach reduces wall-clock time significantly when the subtasks are genuinely independent and do not contend for shared resources. The key principle is that the overhead of coordination must remain lower than the time saved by running things simultaneously.

2. The most common pitfall in parallel work is hidden coupling between tasks that appear independent on the surface. When two workers unknowingly modify the same file, data structure, or external resource, they introduce race conditions and merge conflicts that can be harder to debug than the original sequential problem would have been. Good parallel design therefore invests heavily in clear ownership boundaries, well-defined interfaces between units of work, and review gates that catch cross-cutting issues before they cascade.

3. Measuring the effectiveness of parallel work requires looking beyond raw speed. A team that ships five parallel branches in a day but spends three days reconciling them has not improved throughput. The metrics that matter are end-to-end delivery time, defect rate after merge, and the cognitive load placed on reviewers. When these metrics degrade, it is often a signal that the granularity of parallel tasks is wrong—either too coarse, causing workers to block each other, or too fine, generating coordination overhead that exceeds the work itself.
