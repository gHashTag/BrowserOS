# Parallel Work

1. Parallel work starts with a boundary that every contributor can see. When the scope of a task is written down before the first edit, the worker can move without asking for permission at each step, and the reviewer can check the result against something concrete instead of debating intent after the fact. A specification does not need to be long; it needs to name the files that may change, the criteria that define done, and the work that is explicitly out of scope. With those three things in place, two people can work in parallel on adjacent areas without stepping on each other, because each one knows where their lane ends.

2. The mechanism that keeps parallel work safe is branch isolation. Each task lives on its own branch, and nothing from that branch reaches the main line until a review confirms that every acceptance criterion is met. This means a worker can experiment, break things temporarily, and iterate freely, knowing that the shared checkout is not corrupted by their attempts. The cost of this safety is discipline: the worker must not switch branches, must not commit, and must not touch files outside the declared boundary. The benefit is that review becomes a comparison against a fixed target rather than a negotiation over what the task was supposed to accomplish.

3. Verification closes the loop. After the work is done, the worker answers each acceptance criterion in turn, stating plainly whether it is met, not met, or could not be checked. This is not a summary and not a sales pitch; it is an honest accounting that lets the reviewer focus their attention where it matters. A criterion that could not be checked is flagged as such, not silently passed over. When every criterion has a clear answer, the review can proceed quickly, the merge can happen with confidence, and the next parallel task can begin without leftover uncertainty from the previous one.
